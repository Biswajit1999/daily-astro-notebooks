# Which TRAPPIST-1 planets actually sit in the habitable zone?

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a rocky planet could plausibly hold liquid
water on its surface, based purely on how much starlight it receives. TRAPPIST-1 is a small, cool red dwarf
star with seven known rocky planets packed into tight orbits, and it's one of the most talked-about systems
for habitability — so instead of just repeating that reputation, I pulled the real orbital and stellar
parameters for the whole system and worked out the habitable zone boundaries myself.

Using the star's real measured luminosity and a standard approximate habitable-zone formula, I calculated
the inner and outer edges of the zone in astronomical units, then checked each of the seven planets'
measured orbital distances against those boundaries.

Because TRAPPIST-1 is so faint and cool, its habitable zone is squeezed into a narrow band very close to the
star. A few planets — matching what's widely reported about the system — landed inside that zone by this
calculation, which was a satisfying check that my own quick math matched the broader consensus rather than
just trusting a number I'd read somewhere.

It's worth being honest about the limits here: "in the habitable zone" only means the energy balance allows
liquid water in principle — it says nothing about whether these planets actually have atmospheres or oceans
at all, which we still don't know.
