# Is Kepler-442b really in its star's habitable zone?

Kepler-442b is frequently listed as one of the most Earth-like confirmed exoplanets known, both in size and
in how much starlight it receives. Rather than taking that reputation at face value, I pulled its real
archive parameters and worked out its host star's habitable zone boundaries myself, the same way I did for
TRAPPIST-1 in an earlier notebook in this folder, to check the claim directly.

Using the star's real measured luminosity, I calculated the inner and outer habitable-zone distance and
compared that range against the planet's actual measured orbital distance, and also pulled its radius and
equilibrium temperature for context.

By this simple calculation, Kepler-442b really does land inside the habitable zone, and its radius (a bit
larger than Earth's, in the "super-Earth" size range) is close enough to Earth-like that the reputation
holds up reasonably well against a quick, independent check. That was satisfying to confirm rather than just
assume.

Worth being careful about: "habitable zone" only means liquid water is energetically plausible on the
surface, not that the planet is actually habitable — we don't currently know whether it even has an
atmosphere, and Kepler (which found it by transits) never measured its mass, so we don't know if it's truly
rocky or a smaller gas-rich world.

**What I'd look at next:** check whether any follow-up mass measurement exists for this planet, since mass
is what really distinguishes a rocky world from a mini-Neptune with a thick gas envelope.
