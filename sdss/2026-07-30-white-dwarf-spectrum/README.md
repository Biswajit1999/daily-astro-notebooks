# Looking at the spectrum of a known SDSS white dwarf

I looked up a real SDSS star that the pipeline classifies as a white dwarf — the leftover, burnt-out core of
a sun-like star after it's used up its fuel — and examined its spectrum for the telltale signature these
objects are known for.

White dwarfs typically show broad, deep hydrogen absorption lines and not much else, because their intense
surface gravity pulls heavier elements down out of the visible atmosphere, leaving a nearly pure hydrogen
(or sometimes helium) layer on top. I marked the expected positions of the Balmer series lines (the
hydrogen lines that show up in visible light: H-delta, H-gamma, H-beta, H-alpha) on the plotted spectrum to
see how they lined up with the real dips in the flux.

The lines were there, and noticeably broader than the same lines look in an ordinary star's spectrum — that
broadening is a real physical effect (pressure broadening) caused by the crushing gravity at a white
dwarf's surface squeezing the atoms together more than in a normal star's much less dense atmosphere.

It was satisfying to see a genuinely distinctive, physically-explainable spectral signature show up cleanly
in real survey data, rather than something you only read about in textbooks.
